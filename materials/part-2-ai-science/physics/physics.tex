\textcolor{maincolor}{\textbf{\faPen\ Open the Scientific AI Laboratory app at \href{https://scientificailab.streamlit.app}{scientificailab.streamlit.app}, go to \emph{AI Tutors}, and choose \emph{Emmy}.}} Emmy is your experimental guide: she helps you set up your research project on exoplanet transits by following the scientific method, one question at a time. \begin{importantbox}
\textbf{Prefer your own AI?} If you would rather run Emmy in your own (perhaps more powerful) chatbot, open the \emph{AI Tutors} page in the app, expand \emph{``Run this in your own (stronger) chatbot''}, and copy the prompt shown there. Paste it as the first message in a fresh chat, and Emmy will guide you from there instead.
\end{importantbox}

Emmy guides you through \textbf{five stages} of the scientific method. Use these stages to structure your investigation.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Research question:} Define one clear question for your experiment. State which variable you will change, and which variable you will measure.
  \item \textbf{Hypothesis:} Predict what you think will happen and explain why. 
  \item \textbf{Experimental plan:} Decide exactly how you will build the model, which planet sizes you will test, how many trials you will do, and how you will keep the other conditions the same.
  \item \textbf{Data collection:} Carry out the experiment and record your measurements carefully. For each trial, note the planet size, the time or position during the transit, and the measured light intensity.
  \item \textbf{Analysis \& conclusion:} Process your measurements by making a light curve and calculating the transit depth, then compare the transit depth for different planet sizes (for example, by plotting transit depth versus planet size squared). Answer your research question using evidence from your data: explain whether your results support your hypothesis, mention any uncertainties or possible sources of error, and suggest one improvement or next step (such as repeating the measurements, testing more planet sizes, improving the model's alignment, or investigating another variable such as orbital distance).
\end{itemize}

\begin{solution}
\textbf{Expected physics (analysis stage):} the fraction of the star's light blocked during a transit equals the ratio of the projected areas, so the \emph{transit depth} is
\[
\text{depth} = \frac{\Delta F}{F} = \left(\frac{R_\mathrm{planet}}{R_\mathrm{star}}\right)^{2}.
\]
For a fixed star, depth $\propto R_\mathrm{planet}^{2}$, so a graph of transit depth versus planet size squared should be an approximately straight line through the origin, with slope $1/R_\mathrm{star}^{2}$. Larger model planets should therefore produce deeper transits.
\end{solution}

\exercisetitle{Calculating the size of your mystery planet}

\question{If you are given a transit light curve of a newly found exoplanet, how can you estimate its size? Use the transit-depth relationship in your explanation.}

\begin{solution}
\textbf{Model answer:} rearranging depth $=\left(R_\mathrm{planet}/R_\mathrm{star}\right)^{2}$ gives
\[
R_\mathrm{planet} = R_\mathrm{star}\,\sqrt{\text{depth}}.
\]
Measure the transit depth from the light curve, use the known (or modelled) star radius $R_\mathrm{star}$, and compute $R_\mathrm{planet}$. In the model experiment, $R_\mathrm{star}$ is the radius of the star-disk image on the phone, and depth $=(\text{baseline}-\text{minimum})/\text{baseline}$.
\end{solution}
